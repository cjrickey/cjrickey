# What Makes a Good OAC Schedule Update (and Why Most Miss)

Every week, on nearly every project, someone has to turn the schedule into a few paragraphs for the OAC meeting. And every week, on a lot of projects, that write-up quietly fails at its one job: telling the room whether the project is still on track and why.

It's not for lack of effort. It's that a genuinely useful schedule update is harder to write than it looks, and the version that's easy to produce is the version nobody learns anything from.

## The update nobody reads

You know the one. It's a list. Activity IDs, percent-complete figures, maybe a paragraph that restates the bar chart in words. It's accurate. It's also close to useless in a meeting, for a simple reason: the people in the room can't do anything with it.

The owner's rep doesn't want to audit your logic ties. The architect isn't going to trace the longest path through a table. What they want to know — the only thing most of them want to know — is: **are we still going to hit the date, and if not, what's the problem?** An update that buries that answer under detail, or never quite states it, gets mentally filed as noise.

## The update that works

A useful schedule update is built backwards from the reader's question. It leads with the answer, then earns it with specifics.

**Lead with status.** One sentence up top: on track, slipping, or recovering — and against what. Everything after that is support.

**Name the work, not the codes.** "The east curtain wall is now driving the finish" tells a story. "Activity A-4471 is critical" does not. Translate the schedule into the language of the building.

**Cover the three things that matter this period:**

- **What finished** that was actually significant — milestones and driving work, not every task that ticked to 100%.
- **What's driving the schedule right now** — the current critical path, in plain terms. Not just that something is critical, but what it is and where it leads.
- **What's coming** that could still move the date — the near-term work with the least slack, so the room knows where to watch.

**Say what the data shows, and stop there.** The strongest updates state facts and let the reader draw conclusions. They don't editorialize, and they don't dress up a slip as "a minor adjustment." Owners can tell, and it costs you trust you'll want later.

## Why the good version keeps losing

Here's the uncomfortable part: most teams know all of this. The good update loses anyway, because writing it every week is real work. Pulling the current critical path, figuring out what's genuinely significant, translating it into plain language, doing it again seven days later — that's an hour of a scheduler's time, or an evening of the PM's, on repeat. When things get busy, the copy-paste version wins by default.

That's the actual problem worth solving: not "how do we write a better update once," but "how do we produce the good version every week without it eating someone's time."
